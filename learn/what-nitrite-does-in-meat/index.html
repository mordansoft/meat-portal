What does nitrite do in meat?

Three jobs: it holds back the bacteria that cause botulism, it gives cured meat its pink colour, and it gives it the “cured” flavour that keeps instead of turning rancid.

How does it protect against botulism?

Clostridium botulinum lives as spores that survive salting and ordinary cooking. Where there is little oxygen, some moisture and enough time — inside a sausage, in the middle of a ham hanging for weeks, in vacuum-packed or cold-smoked meat — the spores can wake up and make botulinum toxin, one of the most poisonous substances known. Nitrite, together with salt and cold, stops the spores from growing and making toxin. The exact way it does this is still not fully worked out; what is well established is that it works as part of a combination with salt and refrigeration, not on its own.

Why is cured meat pink?

In the meat, nitrite turns into nitric oxide. Nitric oxide binds to myoglobin — the protein that makes raw meat red — and forms a stable red pigment. When the meat is cooked, that pigment turns the familiar pink of ham and bacon instead of the grey-brown of a cooked pork chop. Vitamin C compounds (sodium ascorbate or erythorbate) speed this up.

What does it do to taste?

It slows down the oxidation of fat. That is what keeps cured meat tasting “cured” instead of rancid after weeks of drying, and why reheated ham does not get the stale taste reheated roast pork does.

And nitrate (Cure #2)?

Nitrate does not work by itself. Bacteria in the meat slowly convert it into nitrite over weeks. That makes it a slow-release reserve for products that hang for months, such as salami or coppa. For short cures it is useless, and in bacon it is not allowed at all under US rules.

When do I need nitrite?

  • Cold smoking — the smoke stays below about 32 °C / 90 °F and nothing ever gets hot enough to kill bacteria.
  • Air-dried whole muscle: pancetta, coppa, bresaola, dry-cured ham.
  • Fermented or air-dried sausage.
  • Any cure that runs longer than two days.

When is plain salt enough?

When you salt the meat and then cook it: within two days, kept at 4 °C or below the whole time, and finished at a temperature that actually cooks. That covers a brined turkey, chicken, pork chops, or brisket before hot smoking. The brine and dry brine calculators are for these.
